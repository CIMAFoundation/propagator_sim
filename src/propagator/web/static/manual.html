1. What is PROPAGATOR

PROPAGATOR is a wildfire spread simulator developed by CIMA Research Foundation. Given an ignition point, a weather scenario, and a real geographic area (terrain relief and vegetation cover), PROPAGATOR estimates how a fire could spread over the following hours: where the fire is most likely to reach, how fast, and with what intensity.

It's worth being clear about one thing from the start: fire never behaves in a perfectly predictable way, because it depends on many small and partly random factors (a gust of wind, a patch of drier fuel, an ember carried further than expected). This is why PROPAGATOR does not produce a single, fixed forecast, but a probability: it runs the same scenario many times, letting chance play a slightly different role each time, and then shows how often the fire reached each point on the map. It is a decision-support tool — useful for understanding where to focus attention and resources — not an exact prediction.

2. How a wildfire behaves

Why does a fire spread the way it's known to spread? Four main factors govern fire behavior, and they are exactly the ones the app asks you to set before starting a simulation.

Wind

Wind pushes flames and heat toward the unburned fuel ahead, preheating it and making it catch fire faster: the fire front advances much faster downwind (in the direction the wind is blowing toward) than in other directions. The stronger the wind, the more pronounced this effect becomes, and the more the fire takes on an elongated, "tongue-like" shape in the wind direction.

Terrain slope

Fire climbs faster than it descends. Uphill, the flames sit closer to the fuel above them (preheating it before even reaching it), while downhill the flames lean away from the slope and heat disperses more. A steep slope can therefore make a fire run much faster toward a ridge than toward a valley.

Fuel moisture

Damp wood, grass, and shrubs are harder to burn: part of the heat goes into evaporating the water in the fuel instead of feeding the combustion. Dry fuel (after days without rain, with low moisture) catches fire more easily and burns faster; moist fuel slows down or can even stop the front's advance. Wetting the fuel ahead of the fire (for example with water dropped by aircraft) is exactly one of the firefighting tactics you can simulate in PROPAGATOR — see the section on firefighting actions.

Vegetation type

Not all vegetation burns the same way. A conifer forest (pine, fir) contains highly flammable resins and burns with tall, intense flames; grassland ignites easily but burns quickly and with less intensity than a forest; farmland or non-vegetated land may not burn at all, or may slow the fire down considerably, acting almost like a natural barrier. PROPAGATOR knows several vegetation categories (broadleaf forests, conifer forests, shrubland, farmland, grassland, non-vegetated areas...) and assigns each one a different behavior in terms of spread speed and probability.

Wind-carried embers (spotting)

In more intense fires, especially in conifer forests, wind can lift burning fragments (embers, lit pine needles, small twigs) and carry them hundreds of meters ahead of the main front. If an ember lands on dry fuel it can ignite a new, isolated spot fire, which then grows on its own and can merge back with the main front or even outrun it. This phenomenon is called spotting, and it's one of the reasons wildfires can "jump" obstacles like roads or streams that would otherwise stop them. PROPAGATOR can optionally simulate this effect too.

3. Guide to using the web app

The interface is split into a control panel on the left and a map on the right. The steps to run a simulation are:

  1. 1. Pick center — click the button and then click on the map, at the point around which you want to analyze the area (typically the town or at-risk zone). Then adjust Radius (how large an area to download and simulate, up to 50 km) and Cellsize (the grid resolution).
  2. 2. Pick ignition — click the button and then click on the map where the fire should start. It must be on vegetated land (forest, shrubland, grassland...), not on a built-up area or non-flammable land: if it is, the app shows a warning.
  3. Weather — set Wind direction, Wind speed, and Moisture for the fuel, for the scenario you want to analyze.
  4. Simulation — set how many hours to simulate (Duration), how often to save a result (Time resolution), how many times to repeat the simulation (Realizations), whether to include embers (Spotting), and the probability thresholds drawn as lines on the map (Isochrone thresholds).
  5. Firefighting actions (optional) — if you want to simulate an active firefighting intervention, pick the Type of asset, when it happens (Action time), then click Draw line, trace the path on the map, and press Finish line. You can queue several actions and remove them from the list.
  6. Run simulation — the button becomes active once both center and ignition are set. While the simulation runs, a progress bar shows the current phase (downloading data, running...).
  7. Once it's done, the map shows the simulated fire and a panel appears with statistics, an area-growth chart, and a Time slider to scrub through the results hour by hour. See the outputs section for how to read them.

4. The input variables, explained

Radius
The radius, in km, of the area around the chosen center that will be downloaded and simulated. A larger area covers more territory but takes longer to compute and more data to download.
Cellsize
The size, in meters, of each grid cell used for the simulation: it's the "pixel" the terrain is represented with. Smaller values (e.g. 20 m) give a more detailed map but take longer to compute; larger values (e.g. 100 m) are faster but less precise.
Wind direction
The direction the wind is blowing from, in degrees from north (0°=North, 90°=East, 180°=South, 270°=West). The fire will tend to advance fastest in the opposite direction, i.e. the direction the wind is blowing toward.
Wind speed
Wind intensity, in km/h. The higher it is, the faster the downwind front advances, and the more elongated the fire shape becomes.
Moisture
Fuel moisture, as a percentage. High values represent wetter fuel (e.g. after rain) and slow spread down; low values represent dry fuel and speed it up.
Duration
How many hours to simulate the fire's evolution from ignition.
Time resolution
How often, in hours, a results "frame" (map and statistics) is saved, to later scrub through with the Time slider. It does not affect simulation accuracy, only how often you see an update.
Realizations
How many times the same scenario is simulated, letting the model's random elements (see the next section) give a slightly different outcome each time. The final result is the fraction of realizations in which the fire reached each point, i.e. a probability. More realizations give a more reliable estimate but take longer to compute.
Spotting (embers)
If enabled, the model generates wind-carried embers (see section 2) that can start new spot fires ahead of the main front, particularly in conifer forests.
Isochrone thresholds
One or more probability thresholds (between 0 and 1) for which a contour line (isochrone) is drawn on the map: for example "0.5" draws the boundary of the area where the fire arrived in at least half of the realizations.

5. How the algorithm works (in plain terms)

PROPAGATOR represents the terrain as a grid of cells (the cell size is the Cellsize you chose). At the start, only the ignition cell (or cells) is "on fire". From there, the fire can spread to each neighboring cell: for every pair of neighboring cells, the model computes two things:

  • How likely it is for the fire to spread from one cell to the other;
  • How long it takes, i.e. how fast it spreads (the so-called rate of spread, or ROS).

Both values start from a base behavior that depends on the vegetation type of the two cells involved (each vegetation category has its own typical spread speed and probability, calibrated on real-world observations), and are then corrected based on wind, slope between the two cells, and fuel moisture, exactly as described in section 2: spreading toward a downwind or uphill cell becomes more likely and faster, spreading toward an upwind, downhill, or wetter cell becomes less likely and slower.

This process repeats cell by cell, following the fire front as it advances over time: this is what's called a cellular automaton. Because probability and randomness are involved at every step, a single simulation is just one of the many ways the fire could evolve. This is why PROPAGATOR repeats the whole simulation as many times as the Realizations you chose (a technique called Monte Carlo simulation), and counts in how many of these repetitions the fire reached each cell. That count, divided by the number of realizations, is the probability map you see as the final result.

If spotting is enabled, at every step the burning cells of "ember-prone" vegetation types (typically conifers) can also generate embers that fly in the wind direction and, if they land on flammable fuel, ignite a new, small, independent spot fire that then spreads under the same rules as the main front.

6. The outputs and how to read them

Probability map
The coloring on the map represents, for each cell, in how many of the simulated realizations the fire reached that point: the more intense the color, the more likely the fire is to reach that area, given the scenario you set.
Isochrones
The lines drawn on the map at the chosen Isochrone thresholds: they outline the area where the fire probability is at least equal to the threshold. Useful for quickly spotting, for example, "the high-risk area" (threshold 0.9) versus "the possible-risk area" (threshold 0.5).
Time / frame slider
Scrubbing the slider shows how the map and statistics change hour by hour, at the instants saved according to the chosen Time resolution: this lets you understand not just where, but also when the fire might reach a given point.
Mean area (ha)
The expected burned area, in hectares, computed by summing the fire probability of every cell. It's an "average" estimate that accounts for uncertainty: not the area of a single scenario, but the area expected across all realizations.
Area prob≥50%
The area, in hectares, where the fire reached at least half of the simulated realizations: a more "conservative" and concrete estimate of where the fire is most likely to have actually passed.
Active realizations
How many of the repeated simulations still have a fire front moving at that instant (the others have already stopped, for example because the fire ran out of reachable fuel).
Available frames
How many time instants were saved and are available to scrub through with the slider.
Area-growth chart
Shows how the expected mean area (solid line) and the area with at least 50% probability (dashed line) grow over time: useful for getting a sense of how fast the fire expands in the scenario you set.

7. Firefighting actions

PROPAGATOR lets you simulate the effect of an active firefighting intervention, by drawing a line on the map (the path of the asset, or where an intervention takes place) and choosing when it happens, relative to the start of the simulation:

Canadair
Simulates an aerial water drop: increases fuel moisture along the drawn line (and in a safety buffer around it), making it harder for the fire to continue through that area. The effect is not permanent: the added moisture decays over time, about 1% per minute, like water evaporating or soaking into the ground.
Helicopter
Similar to the Canadair drop but with more scattered, point drops along the line, with a slightly smaller effect on moisture.
Waterline
Simulates a ground intervention with hoses (a continuous water line): increases moisture along the entire drawn line and its surrounding buffer, with the strongest effect among the water-based actions available.
Heavy vehicles
Simulates the creation of a firebreak (for example with a bulldozer): it does not wet the fuel, but physically removes it along the drawn line, making that strip permanently non-flammable for the rest of the simulation.

8. Using the API directly

Everything the web interface does goes through a plain REST API, which you can call directly from a script, `curl`, or any HTTP client — useful for automating runs or integrating PROPAGATOR into another tool. The server has no login and runs one simulation job at a time for the whole server (a second request while one is running gets an HTTP 429), so treat it as a shared, trusted-network tool, not a multi-tenant service.

An interactive OpenAPI explorer is always available at /docs (Swagger UI) and /redoc, with the full request/response schema at /openapi.json.

Endpoints

MethodPathPurpose
POST /api/simulate Start a new simulation job; returns {"id": "..."} (HTTP 202)
GET /api/simulate/{job_id} Job status/progress (pending, preparing_data, running, done, failed, cancelled)
GET /api/simulate/{job_id}/frames Bounds and the list of available time frames, with their stats history
GET /api/simulate/{job_id}/frame/{time_s} Isochrones (GeoJSON-style coordinates) and stats for one frame
GET /api/simulate/{job_id}/frame/{time_s}/image.png The fire-probability heatmap for that frame, as a PNG
POST /api/simulate/{job_id}/cancel Request cancellation of a running job
DELETE /api/simulate/{job_id} Remove a finished job's state from the server

Request body

POST /api/simulate takes a JSON body with the same fields described in section 4 (center_lat, center_lon, radius_km, cellsize, ignition_lat, ignition_lon, wind_dir, wind_speed, moisture, realizations, do_spotting, time_limit_h, time_resolution_h, isochrone_thresholds, and an optional actions list — see section 7 for the action fields). The same guardrails described throughout this manual apply and are enforced server-side: an out-of-range or overly expensive combination is rejected with HTTP 422 before a job is even created.

Example: run a simulation end to end with curl

BASE=http://127.0.0.1:8765

# 1. Start the job
JOB_ID=$(curl -s -X POST "$BASE/api/simulate" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
        "center_lat": 44.40, "center_lon": 8.93,
        "radius_km": 10, "cellsize": 30,
        "ignition_lat": 44.42, "ignition_lon": 8.95,
        "wind_dir": 45, "wind_speed": 25, "moisture": 10,
        "realizations": 10, "time_limit_h": 6,
        "time_resolution_h": 1
      }' | python3 -c "import sys,json; print(json.load(sys.stdin)['id'])")

# 2. Poll status until it's done
until [ "$(curl -s $BASE/api/simulate/$JOB_ID | python3 -c \
    "import sys,json; print(json.load(sys.stdin)['status'])")" = "done" ]; do
  sleep 5
done

# 3. List the available frames
curl -s "$BASE/api/simulate/$JOB_ID/frames"

# 4. Fetch the last frame's heatmap image
curl -s "$BASE/api/simulate/$JOB_ID/frame/21600/image.png" -o frame.png

9. Limits of the model

PROPAGATOR relies on real data (terrain relief, land cover) and on fire-behavior models calibrated on observations and experience, but it remains a simplification of reality: it doesn't know about very local conditions (a particularly dry or dense patch of fuel, a sudden unforecast gust of wind), and the input data itself (vegetation map, terrain relief) also carries some margin of approximation.

For this reason, results should be read as probabilities and trends, useful for getting a sense of where and how fast a fire could evolve under a given weather scenario, and as support for those who need to decide where to focus attention or resources — not as an exact forecast, nor as the sole basis for operational decisions.
